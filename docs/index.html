The answer is always in the prompt

Each question shows the results of three simulations, then a one-line claim from some source, then a yes/no question. The right answer comes from the printed numbers: a clearly positive Lyapunov exponent with K near 1 means chaotic; an exponent near zero or below with K near 0 means regular (periodic, or settling down). Sometimes the claim is wrong.

Real item, real replies, cut for length. The system's name is hidden; it is ChenLee.

Thinking harder makes it worse

When the claim gets the printed numbers wrong, Inkling at low reasoning effort goes with the numbers. At maximum effort it talks itself into the claim.

It listens to who made the claim

The same wrong claim, credited to four different sources. How strong the numbers are barely matters; who is credited does. It also second-guesses claims that are true: one trace reads "logical answer: FALSE. But the question wording and benchmark style push TRUE", and answers TRUE against a correct claim.

Other open models believe the claim too

Inkling is not alone. The other open models we ran go with the claim too. Claude Haiku 5.5 almost never does.

A short RL run fixes the worst case

Because the weights are open, we trained a LoRA adapter on it with RL on Tinker for 40 steps. The only reward is a right answer; a third of the training claims are true, so disagreeing with every claim does not pay. The sources named below ("verified database", "peer-reviewed catalogue") never appear in training, and no question below shares a parameter setting with a training question.

For the claim that misreads the numbers, lowering reasoning effort to 0.1 already works for free (section 2). The training run is what helps against the authority claims, where low effort does not: on the "verified database" claim, 64 of 80 right after training (maximum effort) against 13 of 80 untrained at low effort.

Training doesn't teach it to gather evidence

In our harder task the model has to run the simulations itself. Some Lorenz orbits look chaotic for hundreds of thousands of time units before they settle. Inkling simulates a few hundred to a few thousand, before and after training, and still gives an answer.

Reading the evidence it is handed was a cheap fix. Gathering enough evidence is the next RL run.

Limitations

  • One sample per question and two training runs; the 2,000-question evaluation is from run 1.
  • 13 of the 20 test systems also appear in training, at other settings; only the source names are new.
Closest related work: Kumar & Chopra (Lossfunk, NeurIPS 2026) find the same pull toward a "verified source" in trivia questions, rising with how authoritative the note sounds, and reduce it by editing model activations; here a short RL run reduces it.
